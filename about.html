How it works

The sheet turns a difference of longitude into a difference of sun time, and turns that back into a distance. Its own title says so, printed in the corner: Longitude and Time Calculator.

The short version

The sun moves fifteen degrees of longitude every hour. So one degree is four minutes.

If you know when it is noon where you are, and when it is noon somewhere else, the gap between those two moments is an angle. Take that angle and multiply it by sixty and you have the distance in nautical miles. Convert to whatever unit you like from there.

That is the whole instrument.

The three numbers it runs on

15° of longitude1 hour of sun time
1° of longitude4 minutes of sun time
1° of arc60 geographical miles, or 69.16 English miles

Everything else on the sheet is a way of reading those three off the page without arithmetic.

Working it

The map came with two arms pivoted at the centre. You lay one arm along your own meridian and the other along the place you want. The angle between the arms is the difference of longitude. Read it as time at four minutes to the degree.

One arm on Greenwich, the other on New York. The angle between them is about 74 degrees. 74 × 4 = 296 minutes, so four hours and fifty-six minutes. When it is noon in London it is a little after seven in the morning in New York.

That reading is exact. It needs no scale and no ruler, only the angle.

Turning it into a distance

A difference of longitude is an angle, and an angle becomes a distance only once you know where on the sphere it is measured. A degree of longitude is sixty miles at the equator, thirty at sixty degrees of latitude, and nothing at all at the pole.

So for a distance between two places you need the angle between the places, not just the angle between their meridians. The two latitudes give you that. Once you have it, it is sixty miles to the degree as before.

That is the calculation the chart page runs, step by step, with your numbers in it.

Measure outward from the centre

The mile scale along the bottom of the sheet is meant to be used from the centre outward. That is why the arms pivot at the centre.

Along any line running straight out from the middle, the scale is exact. That is the one thing this projection guarantees, and it is the only place the guarantee holds. The other scale along the bottom is a longitude and time scale. It is not a general ruler for the picture.

The common mistake

Laying a straight edge across the middle of the map and reading the bottom scale off it.

That is not what the scale is for, and the answer comes out wrong, sometimes by more than double. Sydney to Santiago reads 13,856 miles that way against a true 6,118.

The distance is not set by the picture. It is set by the gridded network of longitude and latitude behind it. The picture is one way of laying that network out flat. Read the network and you get the right answer. Read the picture and you do not.

What the projection keeps

This is an azimuthal equidistant projection. It preserves direction from its centre, and it rectifies great circle arcs measured from that centre into straight lines. A curve on the sphere comes out straight on the paper and still stands for the same distance. That is a feature of the tool.

Every flat map is a projection of the same spherical coordinate system, the graticule of latitude and longitude. They differ in where they put the stretching, not in what they are drawing. Discredit one on those grounds and you have discredited all of them, Mercator included.

Try it

Open the chart, set two places, and read the panel. It shows the longitude, the sun time, the angle and the distance, with the arithmetic written out underneath. The worked examples run through the cases in order, including what happens when you move the centre of the projection to the place you are measuring from.
